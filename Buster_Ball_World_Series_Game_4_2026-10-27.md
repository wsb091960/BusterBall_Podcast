# Buster Ball Podcast — World Series Game 4
## Twenty-Seven Outs From History

Welcome back to the Buster Ball Podcast. I'm BillyB.

The World Series is back at Tropicana Field tonight, and Milwaukee can finish the job. Tampa Bay has to keep the season alive.

The Brewers took Game Three, ten to one. They lead the series three games to none. Dustin May gets the ball for Milwaukee in Game Four, with Griffin Jax starting for the Rays.

Let's break down the inning that broke open Game Three, the pitching that made the lead stand up, and what Tampa Bay can change tonight.

## Game Three: when the inning wouldn't end

Milwaukee already had a two-run lead when the top of the fifth arrived. By the time the Rays finally got the third out, it was ten to nothing.

Eight runs. Every one of them scored with two outs. A World Series record.

The moment I keep coming back to happened before the scoring avalanche. William Contreras hit a ground ball, and Tampa Bay appeared to have a forceout at second to end the inning. Milwaukee challenged. The call changed. Christian Yelich was safe, and the bases were loaded.

That is a difficult reset for a defense. You've started mentally walking toward the dugout. Suddenly, you're back in position with the inning's most important pitch still ahead of you.

My takeaway for the Rays is practical: build that reset into the routine. Check the outs. Check the runners. Agree on the next play. Whatever just happened, the next pitch still needs a complete defensive plan.

Brice Turang brought home two with a single. Garrett Mitchell added a run-scoring single. Cooper Pratt drove in two with a double. Sal Frelick followed with another RBI double. David Hamilton and Jackson Chourio kept the scoring going.

The Brewers finished with sixteen hits and no home runs. Tampa Bay managed three hits.

That was the offensive story: Milwaukee kept turning contact into another opportunity. When you're pitching against that, an inning can become dangerous without the one spectacular swing everybody is waiting for.

For Game Four, I'd pay attention to how often the Rays force Milwaukee to begin an at-bat from behind in the count. A strikeout can stop a rally without asking the defense to handle another ball, but trying too hard to manufacture that strikeout can also produce a walk or a mistake over the plate.

The task is to earn the favorable count first.

## Gasser's contribution went beyond the scoreboard

Robert Gasser worked six scoreless innings, allowing one hit and one walk with three strikeouts. He needed only seventy-five pitches.

That's an excellent result, and the modest strikeout total makes the method interesting. Most of those outs required a ball to be handled behind him. Milwaukee did the work.

I'd be careful about turning that into a prediction that every ball in play will keep finding a glove. For scouting, I want to separate the outcome from the process: where the pitches went, which swings they produced, and whether hitters were making the contact they wanted.

Still, Gasser gave his manager something valuable: innings without a growing emergency. A starter who works efficiently lets the dugout choose its next move with more freedom.

Milwaukee didn't need its top relief arms in Game Three. That's the part Tampa Bay has to reckon with tonight. Getting the starter out of the game can be useful, but the Rays also need a plan for the pitchers waiting behind him.

## Game Four: Dustin May and the first trip through

The Brewers have confirmed Dustin May as their starter. He enters with a five-point-six-three postseason earned run average over eight innings, with five strikeouts.

Eight innings is a limited sample. I wouldn't treat that ERA as a forecast for tonight. I would use the opening hitters to assess what May actually has available.

For Tampa Bay, my first question is whether he can get ahead without offering a pitch in a hitter's strongest area. If he's missing early, the Rays should make him come closer to their preferred zone. If he's landing strikes, taking automatically could hand him control of the at-bat.

The approach should respond to the pitcher in front of them.

I'd also resist letting the elimination-game setting speed up every decision. The Rays need runs, but each hitter still needs a manageable assignment: recognize a pitch, decide whether it belongs in his attack zone, and make a committed swing.

For May, I'd prioritize avoiding unnecessary traffic ahead of the dangerous bats. A free pass can change the price of the next hard-hit ball. With Milwaukee's relief options available, he doesn't have to solve the entire game by himself.

For Pat Murphy, I'd judge the next matchup by location, contact, and the hitters due. If those signals turn unfavorable, the decision can arrive before May's pitching line looks especially bad.

## Griffin Jax: interrupt Milwaukee's rhythm

Griffin Jax starts for Tampa Bay. His postseason line is six innings with a six-point-zero-zero ERA. His final four regular-season starts were much stronger: one run allowed in eighteen innings, with fifteen strikeouts and three walks.

Those are different stretches, and neither guarantees tonight's performance. The regular-season finish gives Tampa Bay a reason to believe he can supply useful innings. The postseason sample gives us very little room for certainty.

My recommendation for Jax is to establish a pitch he can land when Milwaukee makes him throw a strike. Once hitters have seen that pitch succeed, the rest of the sequence has a better chance to work.

I'd watch his two-strike execution closely. A chase pitch has to look tempting long enough to earn a swing. If it's being recognized early and taken easily, repeating it can turn an advantage into a full count.

For Milwaukee's hitters, the challenge is to carry over their decision quality. Last night's hits don't give them ownership of tonight's strike zone. Attack a mistake when it arrives, and be willing to pass the opportunity along when it doesn't.

For Kevin Cash, I'd have the relief transition ready before the inning gets crowded. With elimination at stake, a dangerous pocket of hitters can matter more than a conventional innings target for the starter.

## Tampa Bay's offensive choices

Jonathan Aranda and Liam Hicks were out of the Game Three starting lineup against the left-handed Gasser.

Against a right-handed starter tonight, my preference would be to get the strongest available offensive lineup on the field. That's a scouting recommendation; the final batting order still needs to be checked.

And I'd plan the bench alongside Milwaukee's possible pitching changes. A pinch hitter changes more than the next plate appearance. It changes the defensive arrangement afterward and the options available when the lineup comes around again.

For Yandy Díaz and Junior Caminero, the message would be to make a good decision with the opportunity that actually arrives. A walk can keep an inning moving. A well-struck single can give the next hitter a much better situation.

On the bases, I'd evaluate aggression against the pitcher, catcher, score, and next hitters. Tampa Bay needs pressure, and it also needs to preserve the runners it earns.

## The bullpen could decide when the game turns

I'd expect both managers to organize their relief decisions around the most dangerous matchups.

For Tampa Bay, that means being prepared to use a trusted arm when a close game presents its biggest threat, even if the ninth inning is still several innings away.

For Milwaukee, it means staying alert to the same threat. A three-game series lead provides a comfortable overall position, but it doesn't make a particular inning safe.

The useful analytical lens here is leverage: how much can the next plate appearance change the game? Win probability added can help describe those swings afterward. During the game, the managers have to act before they know the outcome.

I'd compare the decision with the options available at the time. A well-chosen matchup can still end with a hit. A questionable choice can survive a hard line drive caught by a fielder.

## BillyB's scouting checklist

First: May's early strikes. Is Tampa Bay getting a pitch it can drive, or letting him take control of the count?

Second: Jax's two-strike pitches. Are the Brewers chasing, fouling them off, or recognizing them early?

Third: Tampa Bay's offensive lineup and first bench move. Watch how Cash balances the immediate matchup with the innings still ahead.

Fourth: the next defensive reset. After a replay, a walk, or a tough play, does Tampa Bay execute the following pitch cleanly?

Fifth: the first major bullpen decision. Which manager identifies the dangerous inning before the scoreboard makes it obvious?

## BillyB's outlook

My lean is Milwaukee. The Brewers have the series lead, an offense producing opportunities throughout the order, and rested relief options. That's a scouting judgment, not a calculated win probability.

Tampa Bay's route to Game Five starts with competitive innings from Jax, better offensive opportunities, and clean decisions when the game gets tight.

I'd like to see the Rays make Milwaukee solve a close game again. That gives Tampa Bay meaningful chances to change the result, one matchup at a time.

Milwaukee is twenty-seven outs from a championship. Tampa Bay needs to make those outs difficult to collect.

I'm BillyB, and this has been the Buster Ball Podcast. Enjoy Game Four from Tropicana Field. Watch the counts, watch the matchups, and watch who handles the next big moment.
