## Narration begins

Welcome back to the Buster Ball Podcast. I'm BillyB.

Tonight, the World Series belongs to Tampa Bay's home crowd. The setting is Tropicana Field in St. Petersburg, and the Rays need a response.

Milwaukee won Game One, three to two, then Game Two, five to four. Two games, two one-run margins, and a two-games-to-none lead for the Brewers.

That distinction matters. Tampa Bay has a difficult assignment, but the first two results don't justify throwing away its entire approach. My scouting question tonight is whether the Rays can create scoring opportunities while making fewer costly decisions around them.

Let's revisit how we got here, then look at the matchups that can change Game Three.

## The series so far

Game One ended with Trevor Megill striking out Jonny DeLuca after a replay had kept Tampa Bay's ninth inning alive.

The lesson I took from the opener was about the price of an out. An aggressive running decision can change the shape of an inning before the next dangerous hitter gets a chance. Speed creates options; choosing when to use those options is the skill.

Game Two brought a different kind of frustration for the Rays. Liam Hicks' two-run homer helped create a four-run first inning. Milwaukee answered, Sal Frelick tied the game with a sixth-inning homer, and Cooper Pratt delivered the go-ahead single in the seventh. The Brewers won, five to four.

For Tampa Bay, the practical adjustment is to keep playing the next inning with the same quality of decisions as the first. An early lead doesn't reduce the value of a routine defensive play, a competitive two-strike at-bat, or a careful read at third base.

For Milwaukee, the useful takeaway is that offense can come from different parts of the order. Tampa Bay's pitchers should treat the lower third as a sequence of real matchups, rather than a place to coast toward the next confrontation.

And for both clubs, two games remain a very small sample. A few hits with runners aboard can swing a series without proving that one lineup suddenly has a permanent clutch advantage.

## Why the Trop matters

The Rays went fifty-five and twenty-six at home during the regular season. Tropicana Field's roof and catwalks also give the venue a distinctive visual environment.

My recommendation for Milwaukee's fielders is to make communication part of the pregame work: decide who calls a high ball, who takes priority when players converge, and who backs up a difficult catch. Familiarity can help the home club, but the visitors can prepare for those situations.

For Tampa Bay, the crowd can supply energy. The players still have to supply the execution. I'd like to see that energy show up in decisive defensive movements and disciplined plate appearances.

## Freddy Peralta: Milwaukee's timing problem

Tampa Bay starts right-handed pitcher Freddy Peralta against Milwaukee left-handed pitcher Robert Gasser.

Peralta's postseason four-seam fastball has averaged ninety-six-point-nine miles per hour, compared with ninety-four-point-three in the regular season.

My plan for Milwaukee's hitters would begin with a clear decision about the fastball they want to attack. Trying to cover every height with the same swing can leave a hitter vulnerable both above and below his intended contact point.

Watch the Brewers' first several swings. Are they getting underneath hittable fastballs? Are they late on pitches in the zone? Or are they forcing Peralta to find a smaller target?

Those observations should guide the next trip through the order. If the fastball is beating hitters in the strike zone, shortening the move to the ball may help. If hitters are chasing above their preferred zone, the adjustment starts with recognition and selection.

For Peralta, my priority would be useful strikes early. A fastball that earns a called strike and a fastball that produces a dangerous swing are different outcomes, even when both appear as strikes in a pitch log.

Getting ahead gives him choices. Falling behind can let a hitter narrow the next decision. Milwaukee should be ready to swing when the right pitch arrives; automatically taking pitches is no guarantee of a productive long at-bat.

## Robert Gasser: earn the next inning

Gasser has allowed one run in nine innings across two postseason starts. His sweeper is a key pitch.

My scouting plan for Tampa Bay would be to establish which pitches Gasser can consistently land for strikes tonight. A hitter who recognizes a breaking ball early can make a much better decision about whether it begins in an attackable location.

The Rays should resist letting the series score determine their swing decisions. Trying to erase a two-game deficit with one swing can make a pitcher look more commanding than his actual location deserves.

For Gasser, the goal should be clean innings with manageable traffic. When runners reach, I'd prioritize avoiding the free pass that turns a single hard-hit ball into a multi-run threat.

And for Milwaukee's dugout, I'd evaluate his next matchup rather than set an arbitrary innings target. If his location slips and a dangerous group is coming up, the best decision may arrive before the conventional late innings.

## Tampa Bay changes the offensive look

The announced Rays lineup starts Ryan Vilade at first base, Ben Williamson at second, and Nick Fortes at catcher. Jonathan Aranda is on the bench.

That creates an important decision tree for Kevin Cash. The starting lineup is only the first version of tonight's offense.

My recommendation is to plan the bench moves alongside the expected pitching changes. If Milwaukee changes pitchers, Tampa Bay should know which matchup would justify using a left-handed bat and which defensive arrangement follows that move.

That doesn't mean pinch-hitting simply because a reliever enters. The score, the base-out situation, and the remaining lineup all matter.

For Junior Caminero, I'd keep the offensive message simple: choose a pitch he can drive. With runners aboard, the temptation is to expand the zone to make something happen. A walk can preserve an inning and pass the opportunity along.

For Milwaukee, I'd make the Rays earn that traffic. Unnecessary walks ahead of the middle order can overwhelm an otherwise sound pitching plan.

## The bullpen decisions

Aaron Ashby enters Game Three with fifteen and two-thirds scoreless postseason innings. Milwaukee's bullpen has a one-point-two-four postseason earned run average.

Those results make the relief matchups central to my forecast, but an earned run average is a record of what happened. It doesn't make the next inning automatic.

For Tampa Bay, I'd look for signs that an opposing reliever is having trouble landing his secondary pitch. If that happens, the hitter may be able to simplify his target. If both pitches are working, the task becomes recognizing the pitch early enough to protect a useful part of the zone.

For both managers, the most important relief decision might come with runners aboard before the ninth. I'd use the strongest available matchup when the game presents its greatest immediate threat, while accounting for the hitters due next.

There are consequences to every move. Bringing in a specialist can change Tampa Bay's pinch-hit choice. Saving an arm can expose a current weakness. The interesting question is what each manager gains now and gives up later.

## BillyB's scouting checklist

First: Peralta's fastball contact. Watch whether Milwaukee is squaring up strikes or merely putting them in play.

Second: Tampa Bay's breaking-ball decisions against Gasser. Are the Rays making him enter the zone, or expanding their swings to meet his pitch?

Third: baserunner preservation. Before attempting the extra base, weigh the chance of success against the hitters still coming.

Fourth: the first major bench move. That could reveal which relief matchup Tampa Bay most wants to change.

Fifth: the highest-pressure inning. Watch when each manager decides that the game needs his best available arm.

For the analytical review afterward, I'd compare strikeout-minus-walk rate, chase decisions, hard-hit contact, and the base-out situations around the decisive plays. Win probability added can identify the biggest swings in game context; it should be read alongside how those plays happened.

## BillyB's outlook

My expectation is another game shaped by a few high-value plate appearances.

Tampa Bay's route back is a strong Peralta start, disciplined decisions against Gasser, and reliable execution behind the pitchers. Milwaukee's route to a three-games-to-none lead is to keep creating traffic and make the Rays defend complete innings.

I'm treating that as a scouting outlook, not a model-generated win probability. The next nine innings still have to be played.

The Brewers arrive with the lead. The Rays have their home field and a chance to change the direction of this series.

I'm BillyB, and this has been the Buster Ball Podcast. Enjoy Game Three from Tropicana Field. Watch the matchups, watch the decisions, and watch what each team does with its next opportunity.
