## Yesterday’s Lesson

Saturday ended Dodgers four, Giants three. Bo Davidson’s sacrifice fly tied it in the eighth, but Kiké Hernández answered with a ninth-inning homer off Trent Harris. Drew Cavanaugh supplied a triple and a double, while Davidson collected two hits. There was some fight in that offense—just not quite enough finish.

My takeaway? Don’t turn that into a demand for everybody to swing harder. I want the Giants creating another scoring opportunity, not trying to manufacture a three-run homer with nobody aboard.

## Today’s Matchup

The listed probable pitchers are left-hander Jack Dreyer for Los Angeles and right-hander Carson Seymour for San Francisco.

Here’s where it gets interesting: I’m preparing for a game of pitching changes, not a conventional starters’ duel. Dreyer has made only two starts among his 66 appearances this season. Seymour worked a scoreless inning yesterday. That’s a reason to anticipate short assignments, although I haven’t verified either club’s exact pitch limit or bulk-pitching plan.

Dreyer’s strikeout rate is 28 percent, against a walk rate just over nine. Subtract those and you get a strikeout-minus-walk rate of about 19 percentage points. That’s the baseball point: he can finish hitters without giving nearly as many free passes. His mix is a four-seamer, slider and curveball, with breaking balls accounting for slightly more than half his pitches.

My offensive plan is selective aggression. Pick a hittable fastball early and commit when it arrives. Don’t interpret patience as taking your best pitch, then chasing something below the zone with two strikes.

And I wouldn’t empty the bench just to chase the opening left-on-right matchup. Build an approach that survives the next pitching change.

## Seymour’s Assignment

For Seymour, the warning light is the gap between strikeouts and walks. His rates are about 16 percent and 12 percent—a margin of only four percentage points. Opponents’ expected weighted on-base average is .380, above their actual .356. In plain English, the contact-based estimate doesn’t suggest he’s merely been unlucky. It’s a small major-league sample, but it argues for caution.

His sinker is his most-used pitch, and half the balls put in play against him have been grounders. I’d lean into that: establish the bottom of the strike zone, make hitters respect a strike, then use the slider and cutter to change their look. Don’t start every confrontation by trying to coax a chase.

If Mookie Betts, Shohei Ohtani and Kyle Tucker are in today’s lineup, they’re the hitters I’d build my matchup plan around. All three played yesterday; I haven’t independently verified today’s batting orders.

My instruction would be simple: don’t give that group extra baserunners. Challenge carefully, rather than nibbling until you’re forced to throw a predictable strike.

As for times through the order, I wouldn’t pencil Seymour in for three trips—or automatically grant him a second. Decide before the lineup turns over whether his command justifies another look. That’s my recommendation, not an announced team plan.

## Bullpen Decisions

So who handles the important outs?

Yesterday, the Giants used seven pitchers. Camilo Doval struck out all three batters he faced; Jason Foley also worked a scoreless inning. Los Angeles used five relievers behind Blake Snell. Both clubs have recent work to account for, but yesterday’s usage alone doesn’t tell us who’s cleared to pitch today.

If Doval is available and his warmup command is there, I’d consider him for the most dangerous pocket in a close game—even if that arrives before the ninth. Don’t save your preferred matchup while the lead disappears.

There’s a potential additional option: Seth Lonsway was recalled Saturday, with Yunior Marte optioned. I haven’t confirmed Lonsway’s assignment today.

On the injury side, Blade Tidwell is on the 60-day injured list with a right elbow injury.

## Scout’s Notebook

Three tactical keys for me.

First, win the strike-zone battle. Giants hitters should hunt a pitch they can drive; Giants pitchers should avoid making every out a full-count negotiation.

Second, anticipate the handoffs. Have the next arm ready before trouble becomes a crooked number, and preserve useful bench choices for the later matchups.

Third, protect every out. Take the extra base when the read is clear—not because you’re desperate to make something happen.

My notebook question is whether the Giants can execute that plan when the game speeds up. I’m watching swing decisions, first-pitch command and the timing of the bullpen moves more than any single radar-gun reading.

## Road to 100

With one game left, the Dodgers’ road to 100 comes down to today. The Giants can finish with 66 wins, and 100 losses are mathematically off the table. Not the season Giants fans wanted—but denying the rival its milestone would be a satisfying last act.

## The Pick

I’m taking the Dodgers, five to three. My lean is that San Francisco won’t get enough clean innings to keep this comfortable.

The Giants’ route to proving me wrong? Score before the pitching carousel settles in, avoid the free passes, and use the best available arm when the game actually hangs in the balance. Give the crowd a close one late, and let’s see who earns that final out.
