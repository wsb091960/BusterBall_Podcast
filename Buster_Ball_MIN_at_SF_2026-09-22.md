# BUSTER BALL PODCAST
## September 22, 2026

Giants fans, tonight’s challenge is simple: don’t let a power pitcher turn your strike zone into his playground. There’s a series to win at Oracle Park, and I want to see disciplined swings—not just determined ones.

I’m Billy B. It’s Tuesday, September twenty-second. The Giants host Minnesota at 6:45 Pacific, with Anthony Molina and Taj Bradley listed as the probable starters. San Francisco comes in at 65 and 92; Minnesota is 73 and 84.

## Last Night’s Carryover

Monday brought a 5–2 Giants win. Drew Gilbert delivered three hits, including a homer, and drove in three. Brett Harris knocked in the other two runs. Bo Davidson added his first big-league hit and a stolen base. That’s the kind of night that makes you want another look at these guys.

The concern: Blade Tidwell exited with right elbow discomfort. An MRI was planned for today; I haven’t verified a result.

So enjoy the win without guessing about the diagnosis. Those are two separate stories.

## The Bradley Test

Here’s where it gets interesting. Bradley brings a 3.81 ERA and 199 strikeouts into this matchup. His strikeout rate minus his walk rate sits at 17 percentage points. Translation: he creates plenty of outs without asking his defense for help, but he isn’t immune to giving away baserunners.

His four-seamer averages about 97 miles an hour, with a cutter, a hard splitter and a curve behind it. My approach would be to get ready early for the fastball, then refuse to rescue pitches underneath the zone. Being patient doesn’t mean watching a hittable first pitch go by. It means knowing which pitch you’re willing to attack.

There’s an opening when hitters do connect: Bradley’s allowed a 45.2 percent hard-hit rate. My read is that the Giants shouldn’t try to manufacture some perfect, ten-pitch at-bat every time. Hunt a strike you can drive. If it doesn’t arrive, take the walk.

Gilbert and Davidson are my offensive watch points if they’re in tonight’s lineup. Can Gilbert stay selective rather than chase another homer? Can Davidson make the adjustment once Minnesota changes how it pitches him? I haven’t verified a final batting order, so those are players to watch—not announced lineup positions.

## Molina’s Assignment

Molina’s season ERA is 4.18 over just 28 major-league innings. That’s a small sample, but there’s something useful underneath: a 5.3 percent walk rate and a strikeout-minus-walk rate of 16.6 percentage points. My takeaway is encouraging but cautious. He’s giving himself a chance by limiting the freebies; that’s not yet proof he can sustain it over a full season.

His fastball averages roughly 96, and the changeup comes in about nine miles an hour slower. I’d establish enough fastball strikes to make that changeup matter, then vary the sequence rather than letting hitters sit on one speed.

For Minnesota, Luke Keaschall’s .367 on-base percentage and Kody Clemens’ 26 homers give us two different problems: somebody who can keep an inning moving, and somebody who can cash it in quickly. If they start, I’d challenge Keaschall carefully without nibbling, and make Clemens earn his damage without a walk in front of him.

My times-through-the-order plan is conditional. Let Molina work through two trips if the command holds. Before the third, look at the actual swings: are hitters taking the changeup, fouling off the fastball and forcing him back over the plate? If so, have help ready. Don’t wait for a crooked number to confirm what the at-bats already told you.

## Bullpen Decisions

Last night, Tristan Beck supplied two and two-thirds innings; Sam Hentges, Jason Foley and Trent Harris each worked one.

That makes Beck the arm I’d be most cautious about asking for length again. That’s my workload assessment, not an official availability designation. I haven’t verified the full recent pitch-count ledger or tonight’s bullpen restrictions.

I’d also resist assigning every reliever an inning before the game starts. If Minnesota brings its toughest available hitters up with runners aboard in the seventh, that’s your high-leverage moment. Use the best available matchup there. Saving your best option for a ninth inning you might never lead is no bargain.

The objective isn’t to pull Molina early. It’s to avoid pulling him late.

## Scout’s Notebook

The roster changed Monday: Jung Hoo Lee went on the injured list with a groin contusion; Bryce Eldridge went on the concussion list.

That reinforces why I’m treating the batting order as unsettled rather than penciling familiar names into it.

My notebook tonight has two questions. Do Giants hitters make Bradley bring his secondary pitches up? And does Molina maintain his location when Minnesota gets its second look? Those answers will tell me more than one loud out or one softly hit single.

## Three Tactical Keys

First, **own the bottom of the zone**. My instruction against Bradley: don’t turn a splitter below the knees into his easiest strikeout.

Second, **make Minnesota build its innings**. For Molina, I’d prioritize strikes with intent—not center-cut strikes, and not four pitches spent avoiding contact.

Third, **manage the first real crisis**. Have the bullpen moving before the game gets away, and choose the reliever for the hitters coming up, not just the inning on the scoreboard.

## Road to 100

A quick Road to 100 update: at 65 and 92, with five games left in a 162-game season, neither 100 wins nor 100 losses is possible. The finishing range is 65 to 70 wins, and 92 to 97 losses. That’s arithmetic, not a celebration. The useful question now is who gives you reasons to believe in next season.

## Billy B’s Pick

I’m taking Minnesota, 4–3. That’s a forecast, not a certainty. I give Bradley the edge, and I’m wary of asking the Giants’ relief group to cover another long night.

But the path to proving me wrong is clear: make Bradley work inside your strike zone, get an efficient start from Molina, and win the biggest matchup before the ninth. I’d happily trade a correct prediction for a Giants series win.
