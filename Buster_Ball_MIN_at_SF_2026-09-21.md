## Turning the Page

Sunday’s 3–1 loss completed a Dodgers sweep, despite five scoreless innings and six strikeouts from Matt Wilkinson.

That’s the frustration: a young starter gives you something worth building on, and you still leave wanting more. My takeaway isn’t to abandon the development work. It’s to demand better support around it.

Victor Bericoto supplied Sunday’s RBI single and went three-for-eight in the series. He’s a bat I’m watching tonight, provided he’s in the lineup.

## Tonight’s Matchup

Here’s where it gets interesting. Tidwell’s coming off six and two-thirds innings of two-run ball against St. Louis, with just one walk. That’s a useful model for tonight.

His season strikeout rate is 18.9 percent, against a 9.5 percent walk rate. That leaves a strikeout-minus-walk rate of roughly nine percentage points. My read: he can’t afford to make every inning an escape act. His four-seamer and sweeper account for about two-thirds of his pitches, so establishing both early would be my priority.

I’d start by making Minnesota defend the strike zone. Get the fastball to a useful edge, show the sweeper for a strike, then expand when ahead. If hitters refuse to chase, don’t respond by throwing the same breaking ball farther outside. Change the location or the pitch.

Brooks Lee is the Minnesota hitter on my radar after going eight-for-seventeen against the Angels.

That’s a hot series, not a reason to pitch scared. Against him, I’d avoid repeating the same first-pitch look and make him earn his way aboard rather than offering a cautious walk.

Now, Matthews isn’t untouchable. His hard-hit rate allowed is 42.2 percent, and his barrel rate allowed is 10.8 percent. Both sit above the league averages displayed alongside them. To me, that says there are mistakes worth attacking—not that every swing should aim for the seats.

Matthews uses six pitches, led by the four-seamer and slider. My offensive plan: narrow the hunt. Look for a fastball in a specific part of the zone early, and let an unhelpful breaking ball go. Don’t turn patience into automatically taking strike one.

## The Bullpen Decision

So what does that mean once Tidwell gets through the lineup twice?

For me, the third trip is a decision point, not a reward. If his fastball location holds and he can still land the sweeper, give him room. If he’s falling behind and leaving pitches over the plate, have somebody moving before the tying run reaches base.

The relief picture needs care. Sam Hentges recently returned from a forearm strain, while Reiver Sanmartin went on the injured list with an oblique strain.

Carson Seymour, Hentges and Trent Harris all pitched Sunday.

I couldn’t verify a complete, current workload-and-availability sheet for either bullpen, so I won’t call those arms fresh or promise anyone a particular inning.

My preference is to identify the best available matchup before the game and use it when the game demands it. Two runners aboard in the sixth can matter more than a clean ninth. Don’t save your best available option for a save opportunity that never arrives.

And with Hentges, I’d want confirmation that he’s ready before planning around him. Being on the active roster isn’t the same as being available for every assignment.

## Scout’s Notebook

Three tactical keys tonight.

First, **make strike one useful**. I want Tidwell ahead without giving away the middle of the plate. The test is whether he can follow that first strike with another competitive pitch.

Second, **hunt a location against Matthews**. Be ready to hit your pitch, even early. But don’t let a runner in scoring position convince you to chase something you’d normally take.

Third, **manage the dangerous inning, not the inning number**. Watch command, contact and the next hitters due up. Make the pitching change while there’s still a lead to protect.

My notebook question is simple: can the Giants repeat good decisions when the game gets uncomfortable? That’s what I’m evaluating—not just the final line.
