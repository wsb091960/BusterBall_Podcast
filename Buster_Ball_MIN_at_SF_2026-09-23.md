## Last Night’s Lesson

Tuesday’s 3–2 loss wasn’t on the starting pitching. Anthony Molina delivered six innings, allowed two runs, and struck out six. But Minnesota turned the game in the sixth with Luke Keaschall’s triple and Brooks Lee’s go-ahead single. Walker Jenkins then homered off Duncan Davitt, and the Giants’ ninth-inning push fell one run short. San Francisco enters today 65–93; Minnesota is 74–84.

My takeaway? You don’t need to dominate every inning to win this matchup. You do need to recognize the inning that’s getting away from you—and stop it before one run becomes three.

## The Lefty Matchup

The probable starters are Cesar Perdomo for San Francisco and Connor Prielipp for Minnesota, both left-handers. These are probable assignments, not guarantees against a late change.

Perdomo brings a 3.72 ERA, but that’s only nineteen and a third big-league innings. Twelve strikeouts against seven walks tells me we should be careful about treating that ERA as an established level of performance.

Here’s where it gets interesting: his strikeout rate minus his walk rate is only about six percentage points, and opponents have hit nearly 47 percent of their batted balls hard. My read is that he needs clean defense and disciplined location more than he needs to chase a big strikeout total.

I’d ask Perdomo to establish strikes without giving away the middle. When he gets ahead, expand with purpose. When he falls behind, don’t turn a manageable count into a walk by trying to make the perfect pitch.

Prielipp’s 4.94 ERA looks inviting, but don’t confuse that with an easy afternoon. His four-seamer averages almost 96, and his slider is his most-used pitch. The revealing split: opponents have slugged .510 against the four-seamer, while his curveball gets misses on about 38 percent of swings.

So my offensive plan is selective aggression. Hunt a fastball you can drive before two strikes. Don’t automatically take the first pitch, and don’t help him by following breaking balls below the zone. Make him bring his best secondary pitches into hittable territory.

## The Bullpen Decision

Now, how do you get from Perdomo to the final out?

Tuesday, Davitt threw 24 pitches across two innings, while Carson Seymour needed five pitches for one inning. Minnesota used four relievers; Jeff Hoffman threw 24 pitches. That’s workload information, not a guarantee about who’s available today.

Braxton Roxby was recalled Tuesday, adding another arm. I haven’t verified the full multi-day availability picture, so I won’t call this bullpen fully rested or assign a guaranteed closer.

One matchup option worth considering, if available, is Trent Harris. His current strikeout rate is about 32 percent, with a 22 percent hard-hit rate allowed. That’s an interesting swing-and-miss profile, although the contact sample is only 50 batted balls.

My recommendation: have relief moving before Perdomo faces the top of the order a third time. That isn’t an automatic hook after eighteen hitters. It’s a checkpoint. Is he still locating? Are the swings getting better? Has the game reached a spot where one strikeout could change the afternoon?

If the tying run is aboard in the sixth, use your best available matchup then. Don’t save the right pitcher for a ninth inning you might never reach with a lead.

## Three Tactical Keys

First, **attack the right strike**. I want aggressive swings at hittable fastballs, not aggressive swings at everything.

Second, **make Perdomo’s contact manageable**. Avoid free passes, finish routine defensive plays, and don’t give Minnesota extra chances.

Third, **manage the threat, not the inning number**. Be ready for that third trip through the lineup, and spend your best available relief arm when the game demands it.

## Scout’s Notebook

My notebook has three questions today. Does Perdomo repeat his delivery when runners reach? Can the Giants recognize Prielipp’s breaking ball early enough to leave it alone? And do the young hitters make an adjustment between their first and second at-bats?

Those answers would tell me more than one bloop single or one loud out.
