# Buster Ball Podcast: Giants at Dodgers Game-Day Scouting Report

## Friday, September 18, 2026

Welcome to the Buster Ball Podcast. I’m Billy B, and tonight the San Francisco Giants open a three-game weekend series against the Los Angeles Dodgers at Dodger Stadium.

On paper, this is a mismatch. The Dodgers come in at 93 and 60, leading the National League West and playing 47 and 28 at home. The Giants are 64 and 89, including 28 and 50 away from Oracle Park.

But rivalry baseball does not always follow the standings. San Francisco leads the season series four games to three, and the Giants arrive in Los Angeles after consecutive wins in St. Louis. The Dodgers have the superior roster and the far greater margin for error. The Giants, however, have already demonstrated that they can disrupt this club when they pitch cleanly and turn the game into a sequence of small, high-leverage decisions.

Tonight’s pitching matchup is César Perdomo for San Francisco against Tyler Glasnow for Los Angeles.

Perdomo enters at 0 and 1 with a 3.68 earned-run average. Glasnow is 4 and 1 with a 3.50 ERA. Those earned-run averages look similar, but the paths behind them are very different. Glasnow owns the established swing-and-miss profile. Perdomo’s assignment is less about matching strikeout totals and more about controlling counts, limiting free bases, and preventing the Dodgers from stacking extra-base damage.

## The Glasnow Problem

For the Giants, everything begins with surviving Tyler Glasnow’s fastball and breaking-ball combination.

Glasnow can win above the barrel with velocity and perceived rise, then send the breaking ball below the zone from a similar visual tunnel. That creates the classic vertical decision problem: the hitter must cover the elevated fastball without expanding down for the curve.

The Giants cannot solve that problem by simply deciding to be aggressive. They need selective aggression. A hittable fastball early in the count may be the best pitch of the plate appearance. But a fastball at the letters is not the same thing as a fastball at the belt, and a breaking ball that begins near the lower edge may never finish in the strike zone.

The offensive objective should be to force Glasnow into the middle third of the plate. That means resisting chase, making him throw competitive strikes, and avoiding empty swings that produce easy two-strike counts. Even if the Giants do not draw several walks, longer plate appearances can raise his pitch count and create earlier access to the Los Angeles bullpen.

San Francisco’s lineup opens with Gilbert, Hill, Cox, and Jung Hoo Lee. That group must create traffic before the lower half of the order faces the most difficult two-strike sequences. Lee’s bat-to-ball ability is especially important because the Giants need contact against a pitcher built to generate whiffs.

The ideal Giants inning is not dependent on one three-run homer. It begins with a disciplined walk or a line-drive single, adds pressure through movement or a deep count, and forces Glasnow to pitch from the stretch. San Francisco must create run expectancy through baserunners before it can cash in with damage.

## Perdomo’s Assignment

Perdomo faces the opposite problem. The Dodgers do not need chase pitches to score. Their lineup can punish strikes, control the zone, and convert one mistake into multiple runs.

His first priority is strike one without becoming predictable. Falling behind allows Los Angeles hitters to eliminate sections of the zone and wait for something they can drive. Getting ahead expands the usable area around the plate and gives Perdomo access to softer contact.

The second priority is keeping the baseball out of the middle. Against this lineup, a walk is dangerous—but a centered pitch after the walk is often decisive. Perdomo needs competitive misses: just off the edge, just beneath the barrel, or elevated with purpose. Noncompetitive misses create hitter’s counts. Middle-middle misses create crooked numbers.

The third priority is controlling the first trip through the order. If the Dodgers see Perdomo’s complete pitch mix immediately, the second pass becomes much harder. He should establish strikes while preserving at least one different sequencing pattern for later plate appearances.

Five innings with two runs allowed would represent an excellent outcome. More importantly, it would allow the Giants to reach the late innings without asking an overworked bullpen to cover half the game.

## Three Tactical Keys

Key number one: win the strike-zone margin.

The Giants cannot afford to give Glasnow extra strikes by chasing breaking balls below the zone. At the same time, Perdomo cannot donate plate appearances through walks. San Francisco does not need to dominate the walk column, but it must keep the difference manageable.

Key number two: convert contact into pressure.

Against a high-strikeout starter, every fair ball matters. The Giants must run hard, take the extra base when the risk is justified, and force the Dodgers to complete defensive plays. A productive out is less valuable than a hit, but it is much more valuable than a three-pitch strikeout with a runner in scoring position.

Key number three: manage leverage before the ninth inning.

The most important plate appearance may arrive in the sixth or seventh, not the ninth. If the Giants hold a narrow lead and the top of the Dodgers’ order appears with runners aboard, that is the leverage point. San Francisco should use its best available matchup there instead of saving a reliever for a clean inning that may never arrive.

## The Sabermetric Read

The standings and home-road splits make Los Angeles the clear favorite. A 29-game gap in the win column is not noise, and the Dodgers’ 47 home victories demonstrate how effectively they convert their talent at Dodger Stadium.

But the season-series result matters as a tactical clue. San Francisco’s four wins in seven meetings show that the Giants have found workable paths against this opponent. Those paths are narrow: run prevention, early count discipline, clean defense, and timely extra-base contact.

The Giants should not try to imitate the Dodgers’ offensive model. Los Angeles can survive strikeouts because its lineup creates power and on-base value throughout the order. San Francisco needs a lower-variance game—fewer free bases, fewer defensive mistakes, and fewer innings in which the Dodgers bring five or six hitters to the plate.

The hidden statistic tonight is shutdown innings. If the Giants score, Perdomo and the bullpen must immediately keep Los Angeles off the board. Giving a run back in the bottom half would erase both the scoreboard advantage and the leverage created by making the Dodgers play from behind.

## Billy B’s Prediction

The Dodgers deserve to be favored because Glasnow offers the stronger swing-and-miss profile and Los Angeles owns the deeper lineup. My projected score is Dodgers 5, Giants 2.

The upset path is clear, though. Perdomo must reach the middle innings with the game within one run. The Giants need at least two baserunners in front of their best contact or power opportunity. And San Francisco must force Glasnow out before he controls seven innings.

If the Giants can make this a bullpen game by the sixth, the probability tightens. If Glasnow reaches the seventh with a low pitch count, the Dodgers’ advantage becomes overwhelming.

Watch the first two trips through the San Francisco order. Are Giants hitters laying off the breaking ball below the knees? Are they getting the fastball into a manageable part of the zone? And when Perdomo falls behind, can he recover without placing a pitch in the center of the plate?

Those answers will tell us whether San Francisco has turned a mismatch on paper into another real rivalry game.
